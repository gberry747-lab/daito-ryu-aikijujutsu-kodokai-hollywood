A dojo, in the old sense 道場

There are no shortcuts in Daito-ryu, and none are offered here.

Training is by kata, in pairs, under a teacher who learned the same way from his teachers, who learned it from theirs. Progress is measured in understanding rather than in months, and rank is earned within the Kodokai's traditional system, not sold. What is offered is a mat, a teacher, and an art that has been handed down person to person since the Takeda family kept it for themselves.

幸道会
古流

Koryu the old school

Daito-ryu is a koryu, a classical tradition that predates the modern budo. It is practiced for its own sake, without tournaments, and preserved by transmission from teacher to student.

合気

Aiki the principle

The heart of the art: meeting an attack in a way that takes the attacker's balance and strength at the moment of contact, so that technique needs little force to complete.

礼

Rei etiquette

Bowing, care of the dojo, and respect for one's seniors and partners are not decoration. They are the form in which the art is carried and the reason a small dojo can train hard and safely.

合気

The art 大東流合気柔術

An art of the Takeda family, carried into the modern world

Daito-ryu Aikijujutsu is a Japanese martial art of joint locks, throws, pins and the subtle balance-breaking known as aiki. By its own tradition it descends from the Minamoto and Takeda clans and from the Aizu domain, where it was kept as a private art of the samurai class.

It became known to the world through Takeda Sokaku, who began teaching outside the family in the late nineteenth century and enrolled some thirty thousand students in his lifetime: police, military officers, and a generation of martial artists. Among them was Morihei Ueshiba, whose aikido grew from what he learned from Takeda. That is why Daito-ryu is often called the parent art of aikido, and why its influence runs through much of modern Japanese budo.

The curriculum moves from jujutsu, the physical techniques of control, toward aiki no jutsu, in which the same techniques are done with timing, connection and posture rather than muscle. Together they form aikijujutsu. The Kodokai, the branch founded by Horikawa Kodo, is known above all for the refinement of its aiki: technique that is small, quiet, and difficult to see.

The secret of aiki is to overpower the opponent mentally at a glance and to win without fighting.Takeda Sokaku

Kodokai teaching rests on the principle of softness, ju: yield to force and redirect it rather than oppose it. It is why the art can be studied seriously for a lifetime, and why a small person can control a large one.

Lineage 系譜

Person to person, for more than a century

A koryu is only as real as its line of transmission. The Hollywood dojo's line runs from Takeda Sokaku through Horikawa Kodo and the Kodokai in Hokkaido to the North American Hombu in California.

武
1860 – 1943

Takeda Sokaku 武田惣角

Reviver and disseminator of Daito-ryu

Born in Aizu, a small man of legendary skill known as the "Little Tengu of Aizu". After the Meiji Restoration he carried the Takeda family art across Japan, teaching police, officers and some thirty thousand students in all, and issuing the first teaching licences (kyoju dairi) of the modern era.

幸
1894 – 1980

Horikawa Kodo 堀川幸道

Menkyo Kaiden · Founder of the Kodokai · Eisei Meijin

Born in Kitami, Hokkaido, and a direct student of Takeda from 1914, Horikawa received the art's inner transmissions and became known for an aiki of extraordinary subtlety, expressed through minimal, efficient movement. In September 1950 he founded the Daito-ryu Aikijujutsu Kodokai in Yubetsu, on Hokkaido's Okhotsk coast. Japan honored him with the Order of the Sacred Treasure and, in 1974, the title Eisei Meijin, "eternal master".

井
Hokkaido · after 1980

Inoue Yusuke 井上祐助 · Shimbo Tatsuo

Menkyo Kaiden Shihan · Inoue 1932 – 2023 · Shimbo d. 2023

Horikawa's successors, who held the full transmission (menkyo kaiden) and led the Kodokai from Hokkaido after the founder's passing. The Kodokai's Japan Hombu is led today by Matsumoto Masakuni Shihan, Kai Cho, and Takahashi Toru Shihan, So Hombu Cho, from Sapporo.

師
San Luis Obispo · since the early 1970s

Kiyama Hayawo Shihan

North American Hombu · Senior Advisor

Born in Kumamoto, Kyushu, Kiyama Shihan has taught quietly in San Luis Obispo for more than fifty years and is recognized internationally as one of the most gifted exponents of aiki. The Hombu he built is the oldest Daito-ryu organization in the United States. Its Chief Instructor today is Daniel Kiyama Sensei, 7th dan, Dairi Kyoju.

The dojo ハリウッド道場

A small dojo inside a Japanese community institute

The Kodokai has trained in Los Angeles since 2005, first in Temple City. In 2018 the dojo moved to the Hollywood Japanese Cultural Institute in East Hollywood and opened a Saturday study group in Culver City.

The Institute is a neighborhood home for Japanese and Japanese American culture that has stood since 1947 and has housed a judo dojo since 1951. The Hollywood branch was welcomed into being with an opening training session attended by Kiyama Shihan and senior teachers from the Hombu, and it has kept the same quiet rhythm since: Friday evenings on the mat in Hollywood, Saturday mornings on the west side.

The branch is not a business with a sales counter. It is a group of students who train together under Destrie Roy Sensei, and who train with Daniel Kiyama Sensei and the Hombu instructors at seminars, called kenshukai, through the year. Each September the branch hosts the Los Angeles Summer Kenshukai, which gathers members of the North American Kodokai for a weekend of training.

Roy Sensei has trained in the martial arts for more than fifty years and in Daito-ryu for more than thirty, in Japan as well as the United States. Training 稽古

What a class is like

Classes run much as they do in Japan. Students bow in, warm up, and work through techniques in pairs under the teacher's eye. The pace is deliberate. The detail is everything.

型

Kata, in pairs

Techniques are studied as set forms with a partner: one attacks, one receives and controls, and the roles alternate. There is no sparring for points and no competition. Your partner is a teacher, not an opponent, and the trust between the two is what makes serious training possible.

柔

Aiki before strength

The first lesson is to stop pushing. Timing, connection and the taking of balance do the work that muscle cannot. Because of this the art does not depend on size, youth or athleticism, and it can be practiced, and improved upon, for a lifetime.

礼

Etiquette

Bowing in and out, keeping the dojo clean, arriving on time and showing regard for one's seniors are not formalities added to the art. They are how the art has been transmitted for generations, and they are expected of everyone on the mat.

段

Rank and licence

Students progress through kyu and dan grades awarded within the Kodokai. The authority to teach is a separate, traditional licence, dairi kyoju, granted by the Hombu. No timeline is promised, because in a classical art none can honestly be.

見学

Your first visit

  1. Email first. The dojo shares its mat with a community institute, and visits are arranged by appointment.
  2. Come and watch a full class before you decide anything. Sit where you are shown, and ask your questions afterward.
  3. Wear ordinary, modest clothing to observe. If you are invited to train, a plain white keikogi is the dojo uniform; the teacher will tell you what to bring.
  4. Prior experience in another art is welcome but not required. Sincerity and consistency matter more.
  5. Dues and other practical matters are discussed in person with the teacher.
  6. Leave the phone in your bag. Two hours of attention is the first technique.

Beginning 入門

How to begin

The dojo does not run introductory offers or sign-up drives. It accepts students the way it always has: you come, you watch, you meet the teacher, and if it is right for both of you, you begin.

  1. Write to the dojo

    A short email is enough: who you are, whether you have trained before, and when you could visit.

  2. Observe a class

    Watch a full Friday or Saturday session. You will see exactly what the training is, and what it asks of you.

  3. Meet the teacher

    A conversation after class. Ask anything. The teacher will explain the dojo's expectations, dues and the way in.

  4. Begin

    Bow in. The first years are spent on the foundation, and there is no hurry. Rank follows understanding.

Do I need experience?
No. Beginners start with the foundation like everyone else. Experience in another art can help with falling and etiquette, but it is not required.
Is there sparring or competition?
No. Daito-ryu is a koryu, a classical school. Techniques are practiced as kata with a partner, with increasing intensity as skill grows, and there are no tournaments.
How long does it take to earn a black belt?
There is no schedule and no promise. Rank in the Kodokai reflects understanding and time on the mat and is awarded within the organization's traditional grading system. Students who ask this question first are usually asking the wrong question.
What does it cost?
Dues are modest and are discussed in person when you visit. There are no packages to buy.
Is the art related to aikido?
Yes. Morihei Ueshiba, the founder of aikido, was a student of Takeda Sokaku, and aikido grew from Daito-ryu. Daito-ryu remains a jujutsu system: it keeps the locks, pins and aiki that aikido reshaped.
Where do I park?
The Hollywood Japanese Cultural Institute has a private lot on site, and the building is wheelchair accessible.
